⚖️ The General Balance Equation FOUNDATION ▶
Conservation Law
In = Out + Generation − Consumption − Accumulation
Every material and energy balance in chemical engineering descends from a single bookkeeping statement applied to a defined control volume. Choose a basis, draw the boundary, then account for everything that crosses it.
▸ THE GENERAL BALANCE
Accumulation = In − Out + Generation − Consumption

Steady state  →  Accumulation = 0
Non-reactive  →  Generation = Consumption = 0
  ⇒ In = Out   (total mass always conserved)
▸ BALANCE TYPES
QuantityConserved?Notes
Total massAlwaysNo generation term (non-nuclear)
Total molesNot alwaysChanges when moles react (A + B → C)
Element / atomAlwaysAtoms are conserved in every reaction
EnergyAlways1st Law; includes heat & work
💡 Strategy: pick the balance that gives the most equations with the fewest unknowns — atom balances shine for combustion, component balances for separations.
🔁 Recycle, Bypass & Purge FLOWSHEET ▶
Multi-Unit Balances
Improving conversion and controlling inerts
Real plants rarely convert feed in one pass. Three flow structures reshape the overall vs. single-pass performance.
▸ THE THREE STRUCTURES
StructurePurposeKey effect
RecycleReturn unreacted feedOverall conversion ≫ single-pass conversion
BypassSplit stream around a unitBlend to hit a target spec
PurgeBleed off a recycle loopPrevents inert / impurity build-up
▸ CONVERSION DEFINITIONS
Single-pass X = (reacted in reactor) / (fed to reactor)
Overall     X = (reacted in reactor) / (fresh feed to process)

With recycle:  Overall X  >  Single-pass X
🧷 A steady recycle loop with a reaction that produces an inert must have a purge, or the inert accumulates without bound.
🔥 Combustion & Degree-of-Freedom Analysis METHOD ▶
Solvability
Counting equations before you solve
A degree-of-freedom (DOF) analysis tells you whether a problem is solvable before algebra begins.
▸ DOF FORMULA
DOF = (unknowns) − (independent balances)
          − (specifications) − (given relations)

DOF = 0  → exactly solvable
DOF > 0  → underspecified (need more info)
DOF < 0  → overspecified (possible inconsistency)

Get the Units Right First
Dimensional homogeneity is non-negotiable
Every valid equation is dimensionally homogeneous — both sides carry the same units. More exam points are lost to unit slips than to any concept. Carry units through every step.

🧮 Reactive Balances — Extent of Reaction METHOD ▶
One Variable Ties a Reaction Together
The extent ξ bookkeeps every species at once
For a single reaction, the extent of reaction ξ links the change in every species through its stoichiometric coefficient — collapsing many unknowns into one.
▸ THE EXTENT RELATION
n_i = n_i0 + ν_i · ξ

ν_i < 0 for reactants, > 0 for products.
Molar flow version:  ṅ_i = ṅ_i0 + ν_i · ξ̇
▸ CONVERSION, YIELD & SELECTIVITY

🔗 Multiple-Unit Balances & Flowsheet Strategy STRATEGY ▶
Divide and Conquer
Where to draw the boundary matters most
A flowsheet is a set of connected control volumes. The art is choosing subsystems that expose solvable balances — often the overall process first, then individual units.
▸ CHOOSING SUBSYSTEMS
BoundaryWhy draw it
Overall processRecycle/internal streams cancel — fewest unknowns
Around a mixing pointIsolates a splitter/blender relation
Single unitNeeded once overall DOF is used up
▸ SOLUTION ORDER
1. Draw & label the flowsheet (all streams, compositions)
2. Pick a basis
3. DOF on the overall system → solve what you can
4. Move to the subsystem with DOF = 0
5. Repeat until every stream is known
🎯 Never write balances for every unit at once — pick the boundary with the fewest unknowns and the equations fall out in sequence.

⏳ Unsteady-State (Dynamic) Balances TRANSIENT ▶
When Accumulation Isn't Zero
Balances that change with time → ODEs
Startups, shutdowns, batch operations and control all involve accumulation. Keeping the accumulation term makes the balance a differential equation in time.
▸ THE DYNAMIC BALANCE
dM/dt = ṁ_in − ṁ_out + generation − consumption

Well-mixed tank draining/filling → first-order ODE
Solution often exponential: approaches steady state with τ = V/Q.
▸ CLASSIC EXAMPLE — MIXING TANK
V dC/dt = Q(C_in − C)   →   C(t) = C_in + (C_0 − C_in)e^(−t/τ)

τ = V/Q = residence time = time constant of the response.
🎛️ This ODE is the bridge to Process Control — the same first-order response, now written as a transfer function K/(τs+1).
💨 Air/Fuel Ratio & Flue-Gas Dew Point COMBUSTION ▶
Beyond Just Balancing
Efficiency and corrosion hide in the flue gas
The air/fuel ratio sets combustion efficiency; the flue-gas water (and any SO₃) sets a dew point below which corrosive condensation attacks the stack.
▸ AIR/FUEL & EFFICIENCY
Stoichiometric A/F = mass air / mass fuel for complete combustion
Excess air ↑ → complete combustion but more hot N₂/O₂ up the stack
→ efficiency peaks at modest excess, then falls (stack losses).
▸ THE ACID DEW POINT
SpeciesConcern
Water vaporCondenses at the water dew point
SO₃ → H₂SO₄Raises dew point sharply → acid corrosion
🌡️ Keep stack/economizer surfaces above the acid dew point — cool flue gas too far to recover heat and it corrodes the equipment (a real efficiency-vs-integrity trade-off).
🔁 Worked: Recycle & Purge (Ammonia Loop) SOLVED ▶
Step-by-Step
Why an inert forces a purge
A worked recycle/purge balance showing the inert build-up logic.
▸ PROBLEM & SOLUTION
Q: Fresh feed to an NH₃ loop carries 1 mol/h argon (inert). At steady
   state the purge argon must equal the argon in → find purge argon rate.

1. Argon doesn't react & doesn't leave in product → only exit is purge.
2. Steady state: argon in (fresh feed) = argon out (purge)
   → purge carries 1 mol/h argon.
3. If the recycle argon mole fraction is y_Ar, purge flow = 1 / y_Ar.
4. Smaller allowed y_Ar → larger purge → more lost reactant.
   (Trade-off: inert build-up vs reactant loss in the purge.)
🧷 With no purge, argon has no exit and accumulates without bound — the purge rate is set by the inert you must reject, and it always costs some reactant.

📜 The Laws of Thermodynamics FOUNDATION ▶
First Principles
Energy is conserved; entropy sets the direction
Thermodynamics governs what processes are possible and how much useful work they can deliver. Four laws frame every energy balance a chemical engineer writes.
▸ THE LAWS
LawStatementConsequence
ZerothThermal equilibrium is transitiveTemperature is well-defined
FirstΔU = Q − WEnergy conserved; no perpetual motion (1st kind)
SecondΔSuniv ≥ 0Heat won't spontaneously flow cold→hot
ThirdS → 0 as T → 0 KAbsolute zero is unattainable
▸ OPEN-SYSTEM ENERGY BALANCE (STEADY)
Q̇ − Ẇs = Σ ṁ_out (h + v²/2 + gz)
              − Σ ṁ_in  (h + v²/2 + gz)

Often the KE and PE terms are negligible:
  Q̇ − Ẇs = ṁ Δh
🔑 Enthalpy h = u + Pv bundles internal energy with flow work — it is the natural state variable for open (flow) systems.
🎲 Entropy, Gibbs Energy & Spontaneity 2ND LAW ▶
Direction of Change
ΔG decides whether a process will go
At constant T and P the Gibbs free energy packages the 1st and 2nd laws into one criterion for spontaneity and equilibrium.
▸ GIBBS CRITERION
G = H − TS        dG = V dP − S dT

ΔG < 0  → spontaneous (favorable)
ΔG = 0  → at equilibrium
ΔG > 0  → non-spontaneous (needs work / coupling)

ΔG° = −RT ln K      (reaction equilibrium constant)
▸ ENTHALPY–ENTROPY SIGN MAP
ΔHΔSSpontaneous?
−+Always (all T)
+−Never
−−Only at low T
++Only at high T
♨️ Le Chatelier: raise T on an endothermic reaction (ΔH > 0) and equilibrium shifts toward products — consistent with ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°.
🫧 Equations of State & Vapor–Liquid Equilibrium PHASE BEHAVIOR ▶
P–V–T & Phase Split
From ideal gas to cubic EOS and Raoult's law
Predicting how a mixture splits between vapor and liquid is the beating heart of separations design.
▸ EQUATIONS OF STATE
ModelForm / use
Ideal gasPV = nRT — low P, high T
Van der WaalsAdds a (attraction) & b (volume)
Peng–RobinsonCubic EOS, excellent for hydrocarbons
Soave–RKCubic EOS, common in process simulators
▸ VLE RELATIONS
Raoult's law (ideal):   y_i P = x_i P_i^sat
Modified Raoult:        y_i P = x_i γ_i P_i^sat
Relative volatility:    α = (y_A/x_A)/(y_B/x_B)

α > 1  → separation by distillation is feasible
α → 1  → separation becomes very difficult
🌡️ An azeotrope (α = 1) can't be broken by ordinary distillation — you need pressure-swing, extractive, or azeotropic distillation.
🎈 Real Gases — the Compressibility Factor Z PVT ▶
When PV = nRT Fails
Z measures the departure from ideality
At high pressure or near condensation the ideal-gas law breaks down. The compressibility factor Z corrects it, and the principle of corresponding states makes Z nearly universal in reduced coordinates.
▸ DEFINITION & REDUCED PROPERTIES
PV = Z n R T          Z = PV / nRT

Reduced:  Tr = T/Tc      Pr = P/Pc
Generalized (Nelson-Obert / Pitzer) charts: Z = Z(Tr, Pr, ω)
▸ READING Z
Z valueMeaning
Z = 1Ideal-gas behavior
Z < 1Attractions dominate (near condensation)
Z > 1Repulsions dominate (very high P)
🌡️ The acentric factor ω captures molecular non-sphericity and feeds both the generalized charts and cubic EOS (SRK, Peng-Robinson).
🔥 Thermochemistry — Heats of Reaction & Formation ENERGY BALANCE ▶
Energy Released or Absorbed
Hess's law turns tabulated data into any ΔH
Reactor energy balances need the heat of reaction. Because enthalpy is a state function, Hess's law lets you build any ΔH from tabulated enthalpies of formation.
▸ CORE RELATIONS
ΔH°rxn = Σ ν_i ΔH°f (products) − Σ ν_i ΔH°f (reactants)

ΔH < 0  → exothermic (releases heat)
ΔH > 0  → endothermic (absorbs heat)

Adiabatic flame temp: all ΔHrxn heats the products (Q = 0).
▸ KEY QUANTITIES
TermMeaning
ΔH°fFormation from elements (elements = 0)
ΔH°cCombustion to CO₂ + H₂O
Sensible heat∫ Cp dT — heating/cooling without reaction
Latent heatΔHvap, ΔHfus — phase change at constant T
♨️ Choose a reference state, compute ΔH along a convenient path (state function!), and the answer is path-independent — the heart of every reactor energy balance.
🔄 Power & Refrigeration Cycles CYCLES ▶
Turning Heat into Work (and Back)
Rankine, Brayton & vapor-compression
Thermodynamic cycles are the engines of the energy industry. Each is a loop of four processes analyzed with the open-system energy balance on each device.
▸ THE MAJOR CYCLES
CycleWorking fluidUse
RankineWater/steamSteam power plants
BraytonAir/gasGas turbines, jet engines
Vapor-compressionRefrigerantRefrigeration, heat pumps
▸ EFFICIENCY & COP

♨️ Reheat and regeneration push the Rankine cycle toward the Carnot limit — more equipment, higher efficiency.
🧩 Fugacity & Activity Coefficients NON-IDEALITY ▶
Real-Mixture Equilibrium
The rigorous criterion behind VLE
Phase equilibrium means equal fugacity of each species in every phase. Fugacity is an "effective pressure"; activity coefficients correct the liquid phase for non-ideal mixing.
▸ THE EQUILIBRIUM CRITERION
f_i^vapor = f_i^liquid    (for every species i)

Vapor:  f_i^V = y_i φ_i P            (φ = fugacity coefficient)
Liquid: f_i^L = x_i γ_i f_i^pure     (γ = activity coefficient)

γ → 1 for ideal solutions (Raoult's law recovered).
▸ ACTIVITY-COEFFICIENT MODELS
ModelBest for
Margules / Van LaarSimple binaries
Wilson / NRTLPolar, non-ideal mixtures
UNIQUAC / UNIFACPredictive (group contribution)
🧪 γ > 1 (positive deviation) points toward a minimum-boiling azeotrope — the usual case for ethanol-water.
♻️ Exergy & Second-Law Efficiency AVAILABLE WORK ▶
Not All Energy Is Equal
Exergy measures the useful work potential
The first law counts energy; the second law counts its quality. Exergy is the maximum work extractable relative to the environment — and it is destroyed by every irreversibility.
▸ EXERGY & ITS DESTRUCTION
Flow exergy  ψ = (h − h₀) − T₀(s − s₀)     (relative to dead state 0)

Exergy destroyed  = T₀ · S_generated  (Gouy–Stodola)
Second-law efficiency  η_II = exergy out / exergy in
▸ WHERE EXERGY IS LOST
SourceWhy
Large ΔT heat transferBig temperature gap = big loss
Throttling / mixingIrreversible, entropy-generating
CombustionHuge exergy loss converting fuel to heat
🔥 Exergy analysis finds where useful work is really wasted — often heat exchange across big ΔT — pointing to the highest-value efficiency improvements (ties to pinch analysis).
⚖️ Chemical-Reaction Equilibrium & K EQUILIBRIUM ▶
How Far, from Thermodynamics
ΔG° fixes K; K fixes the equilibrium composition
Reaction equilibrium is set by thermodynamics — the same Gibbs energy that governs phase equilibrium. K links standard-state data to the achievable conversion.
▸ THE KEY RELATIONS
ΔG°_rxn = −RT ln K
K = Π (activity_i)^ν_i        (gas: activity ≈ fugacity/P°)

van't Hoff (T-dependence):  d(ln K)/dT = ΔH°_rxn / (RT²)
  Exothermic → K falls as T rises (fewer products when hot).
▸ SHIFTING CONVERSION
LeverEffect on products
Pressure (fewer moles side)Favors that side
Remove productDrives reaction forward
TemperatureDepends on ΔH° sign (van't Hoff)
🏭 Ammonia synthesis fights this: exothermic (K drops with T) yet needs T for rate — so it runs at moderate T, high P, with product removal (see Industrial Chemistry).
📈 Steam Properties & the Mollier Diagram PROPERTY DATA ▶
Reading Steam's State
Tables and the enthalpy–entropy chart
Steam is the industry's workhorse fluid. Its properties come from steam tables or the Mollier (h–s) diagram, which makes turbine and nozzle calculations graphical.
▸ QUALITY & PROPERTIES
In the two-phase dome, quality x = mass vapor / total:
  h = h_f + x·h_fg      s = s_f + x·s_fg

Superheated: needs both T and P (two independent properties).
The Calculators domain's steam-table tool returns h_f, h_g, h_fg.
▸ THE MOLLIER DIAGRAM

📐 Departure Functions & Residual Properties REAL FLUIDS ▶
Correcting Ideal to Real
How far real properties depart from ideal
Real enthalpy and entropy differ from ideal-gas values. A departure (residual) function is that difference, computed from an equation of state — the practical route to real-fluid ΔH and ΔS.
▸ THE IDEA
Real property = ideal-gas value + departure (residual)
  H = H^ig + H^R      S = S^ig + S^R

Compute the departure from an EOS (e.g. Peng-Robinson):
  ideal part from Cp(T); departure from the P-V-T behavior.
▸ THE THREE-STEP PATH
1. Real → ideal gas at state 1 (departure)
2. Ideal-gas ΔH, ΔS from T change (easy, Cp)
3. Ideal gas → real at state 2 (departure)
State function ⇒ path-independent total.
🧩 This three-step construction (real→ideal→ideal→real) is how process simulators compute every real-fluid enthalpy and entropy change.

🪈 Bernoulli & the Mechanical Energy Balance FOUNDATION ▶
Energy Along a Streamline
Pressure, velocity and elevation trade off
Bernoulli's equation is the frictionless mechanical-energy balance; the engineering (extended) form adds pump work and friction losses.
▸ EXTENDED BERNOULLI (per unit mass)
P₁/ρ + v₁²/2 + g z₁ + w_pump
  = P₂/ρ + v₂²/2 + g z₂ + h_friction

Ideal (no pump, no friction):
  P/ρ + v²/2 + gz = constant
▸ WHAT EACH TERM IS
TermMeaning
P/ρFlow (pressure) energy
v²/2Kinetic energy
gzPotential (elevation) energy
h_frictionIrreversible loss — always positive
💨 Faster flow ⇒ lower static pressure (the Venturi effect) — the physics behind flow meters, aspirators and lift.
🌀 Reynolds Number & Pipe Friction FLOW REGIME ▶
Laminar vs. Turbulent
One dimensionless group predicts the regime
The Reynolds number compares inertial to viscous forces and sets the whole character of the flow — and thus the pressure drop.
▸ REYNOLDS NUMBER & REGIMES
Re = ρ v D / μ = v D / ν

Re < 2100      → laminar   (f = 64/Re)
2100–4000      → transition
Re > 4000      → turbulent (Colebrook / Moody chart)
▸ VELOCITY PROFILES IN A PIPE
▸ PRESSURE DROP (DARCY–WEISBACH)
ΔP = f · (L/D) · (ρ v² / 2)

f  = Darcy friction factor (from Re + ε/D)
L  = pipe length      D = inside diameter
ε  = roughness        ε/D = relative roughness
📊 The Moody chart plots f vs. Re for a family of relative-roughness curves — the classic tool for turbulent pipe sizing.
⚙️ Pumps, Head Curves & NPSH EQUIPMENT ▶
Moving Liquids
Operating point & the cavitation limit
A centrifugal pump runs where its head curve crosses the system curve. Too little suction head and the liquid flashes — cavitation.
▸ NPSH — THE CAVITATION GUARD
NPSH_available = (P_suction − P_vapor)/(ρg) + v²/2g − h_losses
NPSH_required  = pump datasheet value

Rule:  NPSH_available > NPSH_required
       (typical margin ≥ 0.5–1 m)  ⇒ no cavitation
▸ AFFINITY LAWS (SPEED N)
QuantityScales as
Flow Q∝ N
Head H∝ N²
Power P∝ N³
⚠️ Cavitation pits impellers and collapses head — chronic low NPSH is a top cause of pump failure in the field.
🌡️ Fluid Statics & Manometry PRESSURE ▶
Pressure in a Fluid at Rest
Depth sets the pressure; manometers read it
A static fluid's pressure rises linearly with depth. This one idea underlies manometers, barometers, buoyancy and the loads on tanks and dams.
▸ HYDROSTATIC LAW & MANOMETRY
P = P₀ + ρ g h            (pressure grows with depth h)

Manometer:  ΔP = (ρ_manometer − ρ_fluid) g Δh
Gauge vs absolute:  P_abs = P_gauge + P_atm
▸ BUOYANCY (ARCHIMEDES)
F_buoyancy = ρ_fluid · g · V_displaced

Floats if ρ_body < ρ_fluid; the displaced weight = body weight.
💧 Pressure at a given depth is the same in all directions and independent of container shape — Pascal's principle.
📟 Flow Measurement INSTRUMENTATION ▶
Reading the Flow Rate
Most meters turn ΔP into velocity
Head-type meters create a constriction; the pressure drop across it reveals the flow rate via a Bernoulli/energy balance and a discharge coefficient C.
▸ ORIFICE / VENTURI EQUATION
Q = C · A₂ · sqrt( 2 ΔP / (ρ (1 − β⁴)) )

β = d/D (bore ratio)      C = discharge coefficient
Orifice C ≈ 0.6 (cheap, high loss) · Venturi C ≈ 0.98 (low loss)
▸ METER TYPES
MeterPrinciple
Orifice plateΔP across a hole — simple, lossy
VenturiSmooth contraction — low permanent loss
RotameterVariable area, float height reads flow
Pitot tubeStagnation vs static pressure → local velocity
💨 Venturi costs more but recovers most of the pressure; the orifice is cheap but its permanent loss shows up on the pump bill.
🚀 Compressible & Choked Flow HIGH SPEED ▶
When Density Changes
The Mach number and the sonic limit
When a gas moves fast or drops a lot of pressure, density is no longer constant. The Mach number Ma = v/c governs the regime, and flow can choke at Ma = 1.
▸ MACH REGIMES & CHOKING
Ma = v / c        c = sqrt(k R T / M)   (speed of sound)

Ma < 0.3  → treat as incompressible
Ma = 1    → sonic / choked at the throat
Choked:  further lowering downstream P does NOT raise flow
▸ CRITICAL PRESSURE RATIO
P*/P₀ = (2/(k+1))^(k/(k−1))   ≈ 0.528 for air (k = 1.4)

Below this ratio → the nozzle/relief is choked.
⚠️ Choked flow sets the maximum discharge through relief valves and restriction orifices — central to relief-system sizing (see Process Safety).
🍯 Non-Newtonian Fluids & Rheology RHEOLOGY ▶
When Viscosity Isn't Constant
Polymers, slurries, foods & blood
Many industrial fluids don't obey Newton's law — their apparent viscosity changes with shear rate. Handling them needs rheology.
▸ FLUID CLASSES
TypeBehaviorExample
Newtonianμ constantWater, air, oils
Shear-thinningμ drops with shearPaint, blood, polymers
Shear-thickeningμ rises with shearCornstarch slurry
Bingham plasticYield stress, then flowsToothpaste, drilling mud
▸ POWER-LAW MODEL
τ = K (dv/dy)^n
  n < 1 shear-thinning · n = 1 Newtonian · n > 1 shear-thickening
🎨 Shear-thinning is exploited on purpose: paint flows under the brush (high shear) then stops dripping at rest (low shear).
🫘 Flow Through Packed Beds — Ergun POROUS FLOW ▶
Pressure Drop Through Packing
The Ergun equation spans laminar to turbulent
Catalyst beds, adsorbers and filters are packed with particles. The Ergun equation predicts the pressure drop across them, combining viscous and inertial terms.
▸ THE ERGUN EQUATION
ΔP/L = 150 μ(1−ε)² v /(ε³ d²)  +  1.75 ρ(1−ε) v² /(ε³ d)
        └── viscous (Blake-Kozeny) ──┘  └── inertial (Burke-Plummer) ──┘

ε = void fraction   d = particle diameter   v = superficial velocity
▸ LIMITING FORMS
RegimeDominant term
Low Re (laminar)Viscous (∝ v)
High Re (turbulent)Inertial (∝ v²)
🌀 Set ΔP (bed weight) equal to the Ergun drop and you get the minimum fluidization velocity — the link to fluidized beds (Unit Ops).
🌬️ Compressors, Blowers & Fans GAS MOVERS ▶
Raising Gas Pressure
From gentle fans to high-ratio compressors
Moving and compressing gas spans fans (small ΔP) to multistage compressors (high ratio). Because gas is compressible, the work involves the thermodynamic path.
▸ EQUIPMENT BY PRESSURE RISE
DevicePressure ratio
Fan~1.0–1.1 (ventilation)
Blower~1.1–2 (conveying, aeration)
Compressor> 2, often multistage
▸ COMPRESSION WORK
Isentropic (ideal):  w = (kRT₁/(k−1))[(P₂/P₁)^((k−1)/k) − 1]

Multistage with intercooling approaches isothermal → less work.
Watch discharge temperature (limits per stage).
❄️ Intercooling between stages cuts compression work and keeps temperatures safe — the same reason multiple-effect and heat integration save energy.

💥 Cavitation & Water Hammer TRANSIENTS ▶
When Liquids Turn Violent
Vapor collapse and pressure surges

🌡️ The Three Modes of Heat Transfer FOUNDATION ▶
Conduction · Convection · Radiation
Every heat path is one (or a mix) of three
Heat always flows from hot to cold. Design amounts to controlling the resistance to that flow — insulating it, or intensifying it.
▸ GOVERNING LAWS
ModeLawDriving factor
Conductionq = −kA dT/dx (Fourier)Thermal conductivity k
Convectionq = hA(T_s − T_∞) (Newton)Film coefficient h
Radiationq = εσA(T₁⁴ − T₂⁴)Emissivity ε, T⁴
▸ THERMAL RESISTANCE NETWORK
q = ΔT / ΣR      (resistances in series add)

Conduction (plane):  R = L / (kA)
Convection:           R = 1 / (hA)
Composite wall:       R_total = ΣR_i
🔗 The resistance analogy (à la Ohm's law) lets you stack walls, films and insulation into one overall U-value.
🔥 Heat Exchangers — LMTD & NTU DESIGN ▶
Sizing & Rating
How much area to transfer a given duty
The shell-and-tube exchanger is the workhorse of the process industries. Two methods size it: LMTD when outlet temps are known, ε-NTU when they are not.

U = overall heat-transfer coefficient
F = correction factor (1.0 for true counter-current)
▸ COUNTER-CURRENT TEMPERATURE PROFILE

⏱️ Transient Conduction — Lumped Capacitance & Biot UNSTEADY ▶
How Fast Does It Heat/Cool?
When a body has one uniform temperature
If internal conduction is much faster than surface convection, a body heats or cools uniformly — the lumped-capacitance model, valid when the Biot number is small.
▸ BIOT NUMBER — THE VALIDITY TEST
Bi = h L_c / k          L_c = V / A_surface

Bi < 0.1  → lumped model OK (uniform internal T)
Bi > 0.1  → gradients matter → use Heisler charts / PDE
▸ LUMPED TEMPERATURE RESPONSE
(T − T_∞)/(T₀ − T_∞) = exp(−t / τ)

τ = ρ V c_p / (h A)     (thermal time constant)
Fourier number  Fo = α t / L_c²   (dimensionless time)
🔥 Small Bi means the surface film — not the solid — is the bottleneck. A thin metal part in slow-moving air is the classic lumped case.
🪶 Fins & Extended Surfaces ENHANCEMENT ▶
More Area, More Heat
When convection is the bottleneck, add surface
Fins extend a surface to move more heat when the convective coefficient h is low (gases). A temperature gradient runs along the fin, so not all of it works equally hard.
▸ FIN PERFORMANCE
Fin parameter  m = sqrt(hP / kA_c)
Long-fin heat rate  q = sqrt(h P k A_c) · (T_b − T_∞)

Fin efficiency  η_f = q_actual / q_if_whole_fin_at_T_b
▸ WHEN FINS HELP
ConditionFins worth it?
Low h (gas side)Yes — big gain
High h (boiling)Little benefit
High-k materialBetter (flatter gradient)
🔧 Always fin the side with the lower h — that's the controlling resistance (air-cooled exchangers fin the air side).
☀️ Radiation Exchange & View Factors RADIATION ▶
Heat Without a Medium
Geometry and emissivity set the exchange
Radiation needs no medium and scales with T⁴, so it dominates at high temperature (furnaces, flares). The view factor captures how much of one surface "sees" another.
▸ KEY LAWS

Two-surface exchange  Q = σ A₁ F₁₂ (T₁⁴ − T₂⁴)   (black)

View-factor rules:  reciprocity  A₁F₁₂ = A₂F₂₁
                    summation    Σ F_ij = 1

🫧 Boiling & Condensation PHASE CHANGE ▶

Huge coefficients — and a dangerous limit
Boiling and condensation move enormous heat fluxes at small ΔT because latent heat is large. But the boiling curve hides a peak beyond which performance collapses.

🔥 Fired Heaters & Furnaces HIGH-TEMPERATURE ▶

🧊 Transient Conduction — Semi-Infinite Solids UNSTEADY ▶
Heat Penetrating a Thick Body
Before the far side "feels" the change
When a sudden surface change hasn't yet reached the interior, a body acts semi-infinite. This governs quenching, ground temperatures, and short-time transients where the lumped model (Bi > 0.1) fails.

🌡️ Heat Pipes & Thermosiphons PASSIVE TRANSFER ▶
Moving Heat with Phase Change
Enormous effective conductivity, no moving parts

▸ THE CYCLE

→ vapor flows to the cold end (tiny ΔP)
→ condenses (releases heat)
→ liquid returns via wick (capillary) or gravity (thermosiphon).

💠 Molecular Diffusion — Fick's Laws FOUNDATION ▶
Species Move Down Gradients
The mass-transfer analog of Fourier's law
Separations exploit differences in how fast species move between phases. At the molecular scale that motion obeys Fick's laws.
▸ FICK'S LAWS
1st law (steady):   J_A = −D_AB (dC_A/dz)
2nd law (transient): ∂C_A/∂t = D_AB ∂²C_A/∂z²

D_AB = binary diffusivity  (gas ≫ liquid ≫ solid)
Flux driven by a concentration gradient.
▸ THE TRANSPORT ANALOGY
TransportFlux lawDiffusivity
Momentumτ = −μ dv/dyν = μ/ρ
Heatq = −k dT/dyα = k/ρc_p
MassJ = −D dC/dyD_AB
🔬 The three transport diffusivities have identical units (m²/s) — the basis of the Prandtl / Schmidt / Lewis number analogies.
🗼 Distillation & the McCabe–Thiele Method SEPARATION ▶

📈 Rate Laws & the Arrhenius Equation FOUNDATION ▶
How Fast & Why
Concentration and temperature set the rate
Kinetics answers how fast a reaction proceeds — the dimension thermodynamics ignores. Rate laws are empirical; the Arrhenius form captures the steep temperature dependence.
▸ RATE LAW & ORDER
−r_A = k · C_A^a · C_B^b        order = a + b

Zero order:  C_A = C_A0 − kt
First order: ln(C_A/C_A0) = −kt      (t½ = ln2/k)
Second:      1/C_A − 1/C_A0 = kt
▸ ARRHENIUS TEMPERATURE LAW
k = A · exp(−E_a / R T)

ln k = ln A − (E_a/R)(1/T)   → straight line

A   = pre-exponential (frequency) factor
E_a = activation energy   (↑ E_a ⇒ more T-sensitive)
🌡️ Rule of thumb: many reaction rates roughly double for every +10 °C — a direct consequence of the exponential Arrhenius term.
🛢️ Ideal Reactors — Batch, CSTR & PFR DESIGN ▶
Three Design Equations
Mixing pattern decides the size
Every reactor design starts from a mole balance on the limiting reactant. Idealized mixing gives three canonical models.
▸ DESIGN EQUATIONS
ReactorMole balanceMixing
BatchN_A0 dX/dt = −r_A VPerfect, transient
CSTRV = F_A0 X / (−r_A)Perfectly mixed, uniform
PFRV = F_A0 ∫ dX/(−r_A)No axial mixing (plug)
▸ CSTR vs. PFR
For positive-order kinetics at equal conversion:
  V_PFR  <  V_CSTR     (PFR is smaller / more efficient)

CSTR operates at the LOW exit concentration everywhere,
so its average rate is lower ⇒ it needs more volume.
🔗 CSTRs in series approach PFR behavior as N → ∞ — a practical way to get plug-flow performance with stirred tanks.

⏳ Residence-Time Distribution (RTD) NON-IDEAL ▶
Real Reactors Aren't Ideal
A tracer test reveals the flow pattern
Real vessels channel, short-circuit and have dead zones. The residence-time distribution E(t), measured by a tracer test, quantifies how long fluid elements actually stay — bridging ideal models and reality.
▸ THE RTD FUNCTION
E(t) dt = fraction of exit stream with age t → t+dt
∫₀^∞ E(t) dt = 1        mean residence time  t̄ = ∫ t E(t) dt

Pulse (δ) tracer → E(t) directly

🌡️ Non-Isothermal Reactors & Multiple Steady States ENERGY BALANCE ▶
Heat and Rate Are Coupled
Temperature feeds back on the reaction
Reactions release or absorb heat, and rate depends exponentially on temperature (Arrhenius). Coupling the mole and energy balances can produce multiple steady states and runaway.
▸ CSTR ENERGY BALANCE
Heat generated  G(T) = (−ΔH_rxn) · (−r_A) V   (S-shaped in T)
Heat removed    R(T) = ṁ c_p (T − T_0) + UA(T − T_c)  (linear)

Steady state where G(T) = R(T). Up to 3 intersections
→ low, unstable middle, and high (ignited) steady states.
▸ OPERATING CONCERNS
PhenomenonCause
Thermal runawayRemoval can't keep up with generation
Ignition/extinctionJump between steady states
Adiabatic ΔT(−ΔH)·C_A0 / (ρ c_p) — max temp rise
🚨 Exothermic reactors need enough cooling and a stable operating point — a core process-safety concern (calorimetry sizes the hazard).
🧽 Heterogeneous Catalysis — Thiele Modulus & Effectiveness DIFFUSION-REACTION ▶
Does the Pore Interior Get Used?
Reaction vs diffusion inside a catalyst pellet
Inside a porous catalyst, reactant must diffuse in while it reacts. The Thiele modulus compares the two rates; the effectiveness factor says how much of the pellet actually works.
▸ THIELE MODULUS & EFFECTIVENESS
φ = L · sqrt(k / D_eff)      (reaction rate / diffusion rate)

η = (actual rate) / (rate with no diffusion limit)

φ ≪ 1  → η ≈ 1  (reaction-limited, whole pellet used)
φ ≫ 1  → η ≈ 1/φ (diffusion-limited, only the shell works)
🔬 Strong pore-diffusion limitation halves the apparent activation energy and makes rate scale with sqrt(k) — a classic diagnostic of internal diffusion control.
📊 Analyzing Rate Data — Integral & Differential Methods LAB → RATE LAW ▶
From Measurements to Kinetics
Extracting order and rate constant from data
Rate laws are found, not assumed. Two classic methods turn concentration-vs-time (or rate) data into an order and a rate constant.
▸ THE TWO METHODS
MethodIdea
IntegralGuess order → plot the linearized form → check for a straight line
DifferentialGet −r_A = −dC_A/dt (slopes), then ln(−r_A) vs ln C_A → order = slope
▸ LINEARIZED INTEGRAL PLOTS
Zero order:  C_A      vs t   → straight
First order: ln C_A   vs t   → straight (slope −k)
Second:      1/C_A    vs t   → straight (slope k)
📈 The order whose plot is straightest wins. The method of initial rates (differential at t=0) avoids interference from products.

〰️ Second-Order Dynamics & Response DYNAMICS ▶
Overshoot & Oscillation
The damping ratio shapes the response
Add inertia (or two first-order lags) and a process becomes second order — capable of overshoot and ringing. The damping ratio ζ decides how it settles.
▸ STANDARD SECOND-ORDER FORM
G(s) = K / (τ²s² + 2ζτs + 1)

τ = natural period      ζ = damping ratio
Overshoot & decay ratio depend only on ζ.

ζ > 1Overdamped — sluggish, no overshoot

📉 Frequency Response & Stability Margins STABILITY ▶
Will the Loop Stay Stable?
Bode plots and the margins that keep you safe
Feed a sinusoid through a process and it comes out shifted and scaled. Tracking gain and phase versus frequency (the Bode plot) reveals how close a loop is to instability.
▸ THE STABILITY CRITERION
Closed loop is unstable if, at the frequency where phase = −180°,
the open-loop gain |GH| ≥ 1.

Gain margin  = 1 / |GH| at phase crossover   (want > ~2, i.e. 6 dB)
Phase margin = 180° + phase at gain crossover (want > ~30–45°)
▸ WHAT ERODES STABILITY
FactorEffect
Higher controller gainFaster but less stable
Dead timeAdds phase lag — very destabilizing
More lags in seriesExtra −90° each — closes on −180°
🎛️ Margins are safety factors for the model: keep a healthy gain and phase margin so plant/model mismatch doesn't tip the loop into oscillation.

🔀 Multicomponent Diffusion (Maxwell–Stefan) ADVANCED ▶
Beyond Fick for Mixtures
When species diffusion is coupled
Fick's law works for dilute binaries. In concentrated multicomponent mixtures, species interactions couple the fluxes — the Maxwell–Stefan framework handles it.
▸ THE MAXWELL–STEFAN VIEW
Driving force (∇ of chemical potential) is balanced by
friction between each pair of species:

  −∇x_i ≈ Σ (x_i x_j / D_ij)(v_i − v_j)

Uses pairwise D_ij; reduces to Fick for a dilute binary.
▸ SURPRISING EFFECTS
PhenomenonMeaning
Reverse diffusionA species moves up its own gradient (dragged by others)
Osmotic diffusionFlux with zero gradient
🔬 Rigorous distillation and membrane models use Maxwell–Stefan because real multicomponent systems can violate simple Fickian intuition.

🌡️ Reactive Chemicals & Runaway Reactions THERMAL HAZARD ▶

Cooling (linear in T) eventually can't match generation
(exponential in T) → thermal runaway, pressure rise, possible explosion.

💥 Worked: Relief Sizing Basis & Scenario SOLVED ▶
Step-by-Step
Which scenario sizes the relief valve?
A worked walk-through of choosing the governing relief scenario.
▸ THE METHOD
A vessel can be overpressured by several scenarios. Size the PSV
for the WORST (largest required relief rate):

  1. Blocked outlet + continued feed/pump
  2. External fire (heat input vaporizes liquid) — API 521 heat input
  3. Control-valve failure (full open)
  4. Thermal expansion / tube rupture

For fire: Q = 21,000·F·A^0.82 (BTU/h), then W = Q/λ (relief mass rate).
Size the orifice for the largest W across all credible scenarios.
🔥 The governing case is often external fire for liquid-full vessels — always evaluate every credible scenario and size for the largest relief load (API 520/521).

⚖️ Chemical Equilibrium & Le Chatelier EQUILIBRIA ▶
How Far Does a Reaction Go?
K sets the destination; Le Chatelier predicts the shift
Reactions reach a dynamic equilibrium where forward and reverse rates match. The equilibrium constant K fixes the position; Le Chatelier's principle predicts how disturbances shift it.
▸ EQUILIBRIUM CONSTANT
For  aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD :
  Kc = [C]^c [D]^d / ([A]^a [B]^b)

ΔG° = −RT ln K
van't Hoff:  d(ln K)/dT = ΔH° / RT²   (T-dependence)
▸ LE CHATELIER — STRESS → SHIFT
DisturbanceEquilibrium shifts…
Add reactantToward products
Increase pressureToward fewer gas moles
Increase T (endothermic)Toward products
🏭 The Haber process (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃) exploits this: high pressure and moderate temperature push equilibrium toward ammonia while keeping the rate workable.

Overpotential drives the current
At an electrode, reaction rate is current. Driving the electrode away from its equilibrium potential — the overpotential η — speeds the reaction, described by the Butler–Volmer equation.
▸ BUTLER–VOLMER & TAFEL
i = i₀ [ exp(α_a F η / RT) − exp(−α_c F η / RT) ]

i₀ = exchange current density (intrinsic activity)
η  = E − E_eq (overpotential)

Large η → Tafel form:  η = a + b·log|i|   (straight line)
▸ SOURCES OF OVERPOTENTIAL
TypeOrigin
ActivationCharge-transfer barrier (Butler–Volmer)
Ohmic (IR)Electrolyte + contact resistance
ConcentrationReactant depletion at the surface (mass transfer)
⚡ A good catalyst raises i₀, shrinking the activation overpotential — why platinum-group catalysts dominate fuel cells and electrolyzers.

🥇 Electroplating & Electrowinning APPLIED ▶

🔋 Cell Potential & the Nernst Equation FOUNDATION ▶
Thermodynamics of Cells
How concentration shifts a cell's voltage
The standard potential sets the baseline; the Nernst equation corrects it for real concentrations and temperature — the link between electrochemistry and Thermodynamics.
▸ THE EQUATIONS
ΔG = −nFE          E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode
Nernst:  E = E° − (RT/nF)·ln Q

⚡ Faraday's Laws & Current Efficiency CORE ▶
Charge → Mass
How much product a given current makes
Faraday's laws convert electric charge into moles of product — the sizing basis for any electrolyzer, electroplating line or electrowinning cell.
▸ THE LAW
m = (Q·M)/(n·F) = (I·t·M)/(n·F)

F = 96,485 C/mol e⁻     n = electrons per ion
Current efficiency η = actual mass / theoretical mass
▸ WHERE EFFICIENCY LEAKS
LossCause

OverpotentialKinetic barrier (see Butler–Volmer)

📝 Worked: Flash Separator Material Balance SOLVED ▶
Step-by-Step
Splitting a two-component feed
A worked example of the balance method from the Material Balances domain — draw, basis, DOF, solve.
▸ PROBLEM & SOLUTION
Q: 100 kmol/h of 40% benzene / 60% toluene is flashed.
   Vapor is 70% benzene; liquid is 30% benzene. Find V and L.

1. Basis: F = 100 kmol/h.  Unknowns: V, L (DOF = 0, two balances).
2. Overall:      V + L = 100
3. Benzene:      0.70 V + 0.30 L = 0.40(100) = 40
4. Sub L = 100 − V:
   0.70V + 0.30(100 − V) = 40
   0.40V + 30 = 40  →  V = 25 kmol/h
5. L = 75 kmol/h.

Check benzene: 0.70(25) + 0.30(75) = 17.5 + 22.5 = 40 ✓
🎯 Two unknowns, two independent balances (overall + one component) → exactly solvable. Always verify with the un-used component.
📝 Worked: CSTR Volume for a First-Order Reaction SOLVED ▶
Step-by-Step
Reactor sizing from the design equation
A worked application of the CSTR design equation from Reaction Engineering.
▸ PROBLEM & SOLUTION
Q: Liquid-phase A → B, first order, k = 0.5 min⁻¹.
   Feed 10 L/min, C_A0 = 2 mol/L. Want 80% conversion. Find V.

1. Design eqn:  V = F_A0 X / (−r_A)
2. First order:  −r_A = k C_A = k C_A0 (1 − X)
3. F_A0 = v₀ C_A0 = 10 × 2 = 20 mol/min
4. −r_A at exit = 0.5 × 2 × (1 − 0.8) = 0.2 mol/(L·min)
5. V = (20 × 0.8) / 0.2 = 80 L

Space time τ = V/v₀ = 80/10 = 8 min.
🧪 A CSTR reacts at the low exit concentration everywhere — that's why it needs more volume than a PFR for the same conversion.

   entering 25 °C, leaving 45 °C, counter-current. U = 400 W/m²K.
   Find the area.

1. Duty:  Q = m cp ΔT = 5 × 2000 × (120−60) = 600,000 W
2. End ΔTs (counter-current):
   ΔT₁ = 120 − 45 = 75 °C      ΔT₂ = 60 − 25 = 35 °C
3. LMTD = (75 − 35)/ln(75/35) = 40/0.762 = 52.5 °C
4. A = Q / (U · LMTD) = 600,000 / (400 × 52.5) = 28.6 m²

📝 Worked: Reflux Ratio & Operating Line SOLVED ▶
Step-by-Step
Setting up a McCabe–Thiele analysis
A worked example of the distillation operating-line setup from Mass Transfer.
▸ PROBLEM & SOLUTION
Q: A column makes x_D = 0.95 distillate at reflux ratio R = 3.
   Write the rectifying operating line and find its intercept.

1. Rectifying line:  y = (R/(R+1)) x + x_D/(R+1)
2. Slope   = R/(R+1) = 3/4 = 0.75
3. Intercept = x_D/(R+1) = 0.95/4 = 0.2375
4. Line: y = 0.75 x + 0.2375
   (passes through (x_D, x_D) = (0.95, 0.95) — check: 0.75·0.95+0.2375 = 0.95 ✓)

Higher R → steeper toward the y=x line → fewer stages, more energy.
⚖️ At total reflux (R → ∞) the operating line becomes y = x — minimum stages (Fenske); the reflux/stage trade-off is the column's central economic choice.
📝 Worked: Pump Head from an Energy Balance SOLVED ▶
Step-by-Step
Sizing pump work with the mechanical-energy balance
A worked application of the extended Bernoulli equation from Fluid Mechanics.
▸ PROBLEM & SOLUTION
Q: Pump water (ρ=1000 kg/m³) from an open tank up 20 m to a second
   open tank; friction loss h_f = 15 J/kg; velocities negligible. Find
   the pump work per kg and the head.

1. Mechanical energy balance (both surfaces at atmospheric, v≈0):
   w_pump = g·Δz + h_f
2. w_pump = 9.81 × 20 + 15 = 196.2 + 15 = 211.2 J/kg
3. Head H = w_pump / g = 211.2 / 9.81 = 21.5 m
4. Power (at 10 kg/s): P = ṁ·w = 10 × 211.2 = 2112 W ≈ 2.1 kW (ideal)

Divide by pump efficiency for shaft power.
⚙️ Elevation dominates here; friction adds ~7%. Always add the friction and any velocity change — and divide by efficiency for the real motor size.
📝 Worked: Combustion Air & Excess Air SOLVED ▶
Step-by-Step
Theoretical and excess air for methane
A worked combustion balance from the Material Balances domain.
▸ PROBLEM & SOLUTION
Q: Burn 1 mol CH₄ with 20% excess air. How much air is supplied?

1. Reaction:  CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O
2. Theoretical O₂ = 2 mol per mol CH₄
3. Excess air → O₂ supplied = 2 × 1.20 = 2.4 mol
4. Air is 21% O₂ / 79% N₂:
   Air = 2.4 / 0.21 = 11.43 mol
   N₂ carried = 2.4 × (79/21) = 9.03 mol
5. Flue gas: 1 CO₂ + 2 H₂O + 0.4 O₂ (excess) + 9.03 N₂
🔥 Excess air guarantees complete combustion but the extra N₂ + O₂ leave hot up the stack — too much excess air wastes fuel (efficiency trade-off).
📝 Worked: Bubble-Point Temperature (Raoult) SOLVED ▶
Step-by-Step
Finding where a liquid first boils
A worked bubble-point check using Raoult's law from Thermodynamics.
▸ PROBLEM & SOLUTION
Q: A 50/50 mol benzene(1)/toluene(2) liquid at 1 atm (101.3 kPa).
   At a trial T, P1sat = 180 kPa, P2sat = 74 kPa. Is this the bubble T?

1. Bubble-point criterion:  Σ x_i·P_i^sat = P
2. Σ = 0.5(180) + 0.5(74) = 90 + 37 = 127 kPa
3. 127 kPa > 101.3 kPa → trial T is too HIGH; lower T and retry.
4. At the true bubble T, Σ x_i P_i^sat = 101.3 exactly.
5. Vapor comp: y_1 = x_1 P_1^sat / P (benzene-rich, as expected).
🫧 Bubble point: Σx·Psat = P. Dew point: Σy/Psat·P... i.e. Σ(y_i P / P_i^sat) = 1. Try the Antoine calculator (Tools) to get the Psat values.
📝 Worked: Adiabatic Reactor Temperature Rise SOLVED ▶
Step-by-Step
How hot does an exothermic reactor get?
A worked coupled mass-energy balance from Reaction Engineering & Safety.
▸ PROBLEM & SOLUTION
Q: Exothermic A→B, ΔH_rxn = −80 kJ/mol, feed C_A0 = 2 mol/L,
   ρc_p = 4000 J/(L·K). Adiabatic. Max temperature rise at full conversion?

1. Adiabatic ΔT_max = (−ΔH_rxn)·C_A0 / (ρ c_p)
2. = (80,000 J/mol × 2 mol/L) / (4000 J/(L·K))
3. = 160,000 / 4000 = 40 K rise.
4. So an adiabatic feed at 300 K would reach ~340 K at full conversion.

If ΔT_max threatens runaway/decomposition → add cooling or dilute.
🚨 The adiabatic ΔT is the first runaway check (see Reactive Chemicals): if it exceeds the decomposition onset, the reactor needs cooling or a lower feed concentration.
📝 Worked: Pipe Pressure Drop (Darcy) SOLVED ▶
Step-by-Step
Friction loss for turbulent water flow
Combining Reynolds number, the friction factor and Darcy–Weisbach from Fluid Mechanics.
▸ PROBLEM & SOLUTION
Q: Water (ρ=1000 kg/m³, μ=0.001 Pa·s) flows at 2 m/s through
   100 m of 50 mm pipe (ε/D = 0.0001). Find ΔP.

1. Re = ρvD/μ = 1000·2·0.05/0.001 = 1.0×10⁵  → turbulent
2. Haaland f: 1/√f = −1.8 log[(ε/D/3.7)^1.11 + 6.9/Re]
   → f ≈ 0.0183
3. Darcy–Weisbach: ΔP = f (L/D)(ρv²/2)
   = 0.0183 (100/0.05)(1000·2²/2)
   = 0.0183 · 2000 · 2000
   ≈ 73,200 Pa ≈ 73 kPa
💧 Check it live with the Reynolds and Friction Factor calculators in the Tools domain.
📝 Worked: Minimum Stages (Fenske) SOLVED ▶
Step-by-Step
Total-reflux stage count for a binary split
Applying the Fenske equation from Mass Transfer & Separations.
▸ PROBLEM & SOLUTION
Q: Distillation with α = 2.5. Distillate 98% light, bottoms
   2% light. Find the minimum number of stages N_min.

1. Fenske: N_min = ln[ (x_D/(1−x_D)) · ((1−x_B)/x_B) ] / ln α
2. x_D/(1−x_D) = 0.98/0.02 = 49
3. (1−x_B)/x_B = 0.98/0.02 = 49
4. N_min = ln(49 · 49) / ln 2.5
         = ln(2401) / 0.9163
         = 7.783 / 0.9163 ≈ 8.5 stages

→ ~8.5 equilibrium stages (incl. reboiler) at total reflux.
🌡️ N_min is the floor — real columns run above minimum reflux, so the actual stage count is higher (Gilliland correlation links the two).
📝 Worked: Batch Time, Second-Order SOLVED ▶
Step-by-Step
Time to reach a conversion in a batch reactor
Integrating the second-order rate law from Reaction Engineering & Kinetics.
▸ PROBLEM & SOLUTION
Q: 2A → products, −r_A = k C_A², k = 0.05 L/(mol·min),
   C_A0 = 1 mol/L. Time for 90% conversion?

1. Batch: t = C_A0 ∫₀^X dX / (−r_A)
2. Second order: −r_A = k C_A0²(1−X)²
3. t = (1/(k C_A0)) · X/(1−X)
4. = (1/(0.05·1)) · 0.9/(1−0.9)
   = 20 · 9 = 180 min

(Equivalently 1/C_A − 1/C_A0 = k t.)
⏱️ Second-order conversion tails off hard — going from 90% to 99% multiplies the required time roughly tenfold.
📝 Worked: Net Present Value SOLVED ▶
Step-by-Step
Discounting project cash flows
Applying time-value-of-money from Process Design & Economics.
▸ PROBLEM & SOLUTION
Q: Invest $100k now; it returns $40k/yr for 3 years.
   Discount rate i = 10%. Find NPV.

1. NPV = −C₀ + Σ CFₜ /(1+i)ᵗ
2. Year 1: 40/1.10   = 36.36
   Year 2: 40/1.21   = 33.06
   Year 3: 40/1.331  = 30.05
3. Σ PV of inflows = 99.47k
4. NPV = −100 + 99.47 = −$0.53k

→ Slightly negative → reject at 10% (IRR is just under 10%).
💰 NPV > 0 → accept. The discount rate that drives NPV to zero is the IRR — here almost exactly the 10% hurdle, so the project barely fails to clear it.
📝 Worked: Humidity Ratio of Moist Air SOLVED ▶
Step-by-Step
From dry-bulb T and RH to humidity ratio
Applying the psychrometric relations from the Psychrometrics domain.
▸ PROBLEM & SOLUTION
Q: Air at 30 °C, 60% RH, 101.325 kPa. Find humidity ratio W.

1. Sat. pressure (Magnus):
   p_ws = 0.61094 exp[17.625·30/(30+243.04)]
        ≈ 4.24 kPa
2. Vapor pressure: p_w = 0.60 × 4.24 = 2.55 kPa
3. Humidity ratio:
   W = 0.622 p_w/(P − p_w)
     = 0.622 × 2.55/(101.325 − 2.55)
     ≈ 0.0161 kg water / kg dry air

Adding a few percent of nanoparticles to a coolant can measurably raise its thermal conductivity — a nanoscale trick applied to a classic Heat Transfer problem.
▸ THE APPEAL & THE CATCH

💧 Humidity Definitions & Terms FOUNDATION ▶
Ways to Measure Water in Air
Humidity ratio, relative humidity & dew point
"Humidity" has several precise meanings. Mixing them up is a classic source of psychrometric errors — each one answers a different question.
▸ THE KEY QUANTITIES
Humidity ratio   W = 0.622·p_w /(P − p_w)   [kg water / kg dry air]
Relative humidity RH = p_w / p_ws × 100%     [how "full" the air is]
Dew point        T_dp: cool at constant P until RH = 100%
Specific humidity q = m_water /(m_water + m_dry air)
▸ WHY W (NOT RH) FOR BALANCES
QuantityBest for
Humidity ratio WMass balances — dry-air basis is conserved
Relative humidityComfort, condensation risk
Dew pointWhen will it condense? (constant-P cooling)

🌡️ Wet-Bulb & Adiabatic Saturation CORE ▶
Cooling by Evaporation
The temperature a wetted thermometer settles at
Blow unsaturated air past a wet wick and evaporation cools it to the wet-bulb temperature. The wet-bulb depression is a direct read on how dry the air is.
▸ THE THREE TEMPERATURES
Dry-bulb  T:     ordinary air temperature
Wet-bulb  T_wb:  evaporatively cooled (T_wb ≤ T)
Dew point T_dp:  T_dp ≤ T_wb ≤ T   (equal only at saturation)
▸ LEWIS RELATION (air–water)
For the air–water system, Le ≈ 1, so the adiabatic-saturation
temperature ≈ the thermodynamic wet-bulb temperature.
This lucky coincidence is why the psychrometric chart works so cleanly.
🥵 Human survivability tracks wet-bulb, not dry-bulb: a sustained T_wb above ~35 °C defeats sweat-based cooling regardless of shade or wind.

❄️ Gas Liquefaction Cycles FOUNDATION ▶
Reaching Very Low Temperatures
Cooling gases until they turn to liquid
Cryogenics (below ~−150 °C) liquefies air, natural gas and hydrogen. It leans on the Joule–Thomson effect and clever heat integration from the Thermodynamics domain.
▸ THE CORE CYCLES
CycleIdea
Linde–HampsonCompress → cool → Joule–Thomson throttle → partial liquefy
ClaudeAdds an expander (does work) — more efficient
▸ THE JOULE–THOMSON KEY
Throttling cools a gas only below its inversion temperature.

H₂ and He must be pre-cooled first (their inversion T is very low)
— otherwise throttling warms them.
🌡️ Regenerative heat exchange (cold product cools incoming feed) is essential — without it, liquefaction would be hopelessly energy-intensive.
🧊 Air Separation & Cryogenic Storage APPLIED ▶
Distilling the Atmosphere
Making O₂, N₂ and Ar from air
Cryogenic air separation units (ASUs) liquefy air and distill it into oxygen, nitrogen and argon — distillation (Mass Transfer) at −190 °C.
▸ THE ASU IN BRIEF
Filter & compress air → remove H₂O & CO₂ (they'd freeze)
→ cool to near-liquefaction (regenerative HX)
→ cryogenic distillation columns:
   N₂ (bp −196 °C) overhead · O₂ (bp −183 °C) bottoms · Ar side-draw
▸ STORAGE & SAFETY
ConcernHandling
InsulationVacuum-jacketed / multilayer (Dewar)
Material choiceAustenitic steels stay tough at cryo T
HazardsCold burns, O₂ enrichment, asphyxiation
🔩 Ordinary carbon steel turns brittle in the cold — cryogenic service demands austenitic stainless or aluminium (see the Materials domain).
🚢 LNG — Liquefied Natural Gas APPLIED ▶
Shrinking Gas 600× for Shipping
Cryogenic liquefaction at industrial scale
Cooling natural gas to −162 °C liquefies it, shrinking its volume ~600-fold so it can be shipped across oceans where pipelines can't reach — one of the largest cryogenic processes on earth.
▸ THE LNG VALUE CHAIN
Pretreat (remove CO₂, H₂S, water, Hg — they'd freeze)
→ liquefy (mixed-refrigerant or cascade cycle) to −162 °C
→ store & ship in insulated tanks
→ regasify at destination (vaporize back to gas)
▸ KEY CHALLENGES
IssueHandling
Boil-off gasSome LNG evaporates; used as ship fuel
Materials9% Ni steel / aluminium stay tough at cryo T
EnergyLiquefaction is very power-intensive
🌡️ LNG ties together gas processing (Petroleum), refrigeration cycles (Thermo) and cryogenic materials (Materials) — a flagship multi-domain design.

🌡️ Joule–Thomson Effect & Inversion FOUNDATION ▶
Throttling Without Work
Why some gases cool and others warm when expanded
Pushing a real gas through a valve at constant enthalpy changes its temperature. The sign and size of that change is the engine behind nearly every liquefaction cycle.
▸ THE COEFFICIENT
μ_JT = (∂T/∂P)_H

μ_JT > 0  → gas COOLS on throttling (below inversion T)
μ_JT < 0  → gas WARMS on throttling (above inversion T)
μ_JT = 0  → the inversion temperature itself
▸ INVERSION TEMPERATURES (1 atm)
GasMax inversion TNote
N₂≈ 621 KCools at room T — easy
O₂≈ 764 KCools at room T
H₂≈ 202 KMust be pre-cooled
He≈ 43 KMust be pre-cooled hard
🧊 An ideal gas has μ_JT = 0 exactly — the effect is a purely real-gas phenomenon rooted in intermolecular forces. It vanishes as pressure → 0.
🔁 Cryogenic Heat Exchangers & Regeneration APPLIED ▶
Recovering the Cold
Why liquefaction is impossible without heat integration
The cold outgoing product stream pre-cools the warm incoming feed. Tiny temperature approaches (1–3 K) demand exchangers with enormous area and near-zero losses.
▸ EXCHANGER TYPES
TypeWhere used
Brazed aluminium plate-finASUs, LNG — huge area/volume
Coil-wound (spiral)Large LNG main exchangers
Regenerators (packed beds)Older Stirling/Gifford-McMahon coolers
▸ WHY APPROACH TEMPERATURE MATTERS
Liquefaction yield ∝ how completely feed is cooled.
A 1 K worse approach can slash yield and spike specific power.
→ counter-current, multi-stream, minimal ΔT (see Heat Transfer / LMTD).
❄️ Insulation losses that are trivial at ambient dominate at 80 K — the exchanger and cold box live inside vacuum-jacketed, multi-layer-insulated enclosures.
🧱 Cryogenic Materials & Insulation APPLIED ▶
Surviving the Cold
Ductile-to-brittle transition & keeping heat out
Materials that are tough at room temperature can shatter at 77 K. Material choice and insulation design are safety-critical in cryogenic plants.
▸ MATERIALS THAT STAY TOUGH
Good: austenitic stainless (304/316), aluminium alloys,
      copper, nickel alloys, Invar (low thermal contraction)
Bad:  plain carbon steel — brittle below its transition T
      (a classic embrittlement failure mode)
▸ INSULATION LADDER
MethodPerformance
Perlite / foamCheap, bulk storage
Vacuum jacketKills gas conduction/convection
Multi-layer (MLI, "superinsulation")Vacuum + reflective foils — best
⚠️ Thermal contraction is real: a 10 m stainless line shrinks tens of mm cooling to LNG temperature — expansion loops and bellows are mandatory.
🧲 Superconductivity & Helium Cryogenics ADVANCED ▶
The Coldest Engineering
Liquid helium, zero resistance & big magnets
Below a critical temperature some materials lose all electrical resistance. Sustaining that state needs liquid-helium cryogenics — the domain of MRI scanners and particle accelerators.
▸ HELIUM FACTS
He-4 boils at 4.2 K (1 atm); pumping gets you below 1 K.
Below 2.17 K it becomes a SUPERFLUID (zero viscosity).
He-3 (rarer) enables dilution refrigerators → millikelvin.
▸ WHERE IT SHOWS UP
ApplicationSuperconductor / temp
MRI magnetsNbTi at 4 K in liquid He
LHC / fusion magnetsNb₃Sn, superfluid He at 1.9 K
HTS power cablesYBCO, cooled by liquid N₂ (77 K)
💡 "High-temperature" superconductors are high only relatively — 77 K liquid-nitrogen cooling is far cheaper than liquid helium, which is why HTS is such a big deal.
